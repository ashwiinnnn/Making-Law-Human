Origin

Earlier this year, I signed up for what looked like the solution to all my DJ problems: a premium music subscription promising seamless software integration. Three clicks and 120 dollars later, I had a year long subscription and was feeling pretty good about my life choices.

Until nothing worked.

After twenty minutes of rage Googling, I found it. Buried in section sixteen of the terms: integration limited to one specific brand of equipment. Mine was not that brand. Not even close.

The customer service rep asked if I had tried turning it off and on again, then delivered the real answer: it was clearly stated in the terms I agreed to. No refund. My 120 dollars, gone. My faith in the idea that anyone wrote those terms for me to actually read, also gone.

I watched a friend do the same thing with her own contract months later: hands up after the fifth "notwithstanding the foregoing," the leap from "I don't understand any of this" to "so my payment is due on the 15th" landing like a small act of relief, not a formality.

That failure produced a question, not just a complaint: if a legally valid clause can remain practically invisible to the person agreeing to it, where does the failure actually occur? The reader, the lawyer, or the document itself?

This project is an attempt to answer that question with something more durable than a strong opinion. What follows is the experiment, the framework it produced, and the open questions it has not resolved yet.

The Human Law Framework

Six layers a document has to survive.

Every article, clause, and experiment this project produces gets tested against these six questions, in order. Hover a step.

01Notice
02Understand
03Trust
04Decide
05Remember
06Comply

Notice

Can people find the information at all, before they even start reading?

Understand

Once they find it, can they actually comprehend what it says?

Trust

Do they believe what it says, or does the document work against belief?

Decide

Can they confidently act on what they now understand?

Remember

Will they still remember it tomorrow, when the decision actually matters?

Comply

Can they actually follow it, in practice, not just in theory?

The Central Question

Can AI generate a legally correct document that a human cannot understand?

Most work at the intersection of AI and law asks whether a model can draft accurately. Accuracy is close to solved. The unexamined question is whether accuracy and comprehensibility are even the same axis, or whether a contract can be perfectly enforceable and perfectly opaque at the same time.

AI can draft a perfect contract. It will never know what it feels like to be on the other side of one.

This project treats that gap, between legal validity and human comprehension, as the actual research object. The Human Law Index below is a first attempt to measure it directly rather than assume a well-drafted clause and a well-understood clause are the same thing.

Psychology of Color

Blue. The quiet overachiever.

Alert, emphasis, precision, inverted. Blue does the opposite of what people expect a "loud" color to do.

Blue
Pilot observation, not peer reviewed

Blue does not shout, but it gets results. It helps people read faster, remember more, and feel at ease, and in a world of shrinking attention spans, that calm clarity is everything.

“Trustworthy” was not just the top response for blue. It was the dominant one.

The association runs deep: the sky, the ocean, the things that represent stability in the natural world. A blue header does not just organize content, it establishes hierarchy before a single word is read.

Most documents fail because they treat color as decoration, not communication architecture. Use blue for structure. Save red for focus.

Psychology of Color

Red. Alert, emphasis, precision.

If everything is red, nothing is. This is the most misused color in legal design, and the easiest one to get wrong.

Red
Pilot observation, not peer reviewed
0%Highest recall rate of the three colors in this project's own testing
0 m/sFastest median recognition time, internal test set

Red grabs attention, fast. It is broadly associated in the cognitive psychology literature with threat and urgency detection, which is exactly why it is the most misused color in legal design.

Most people think red creates urgency, so they use it for everything. In practice it tends to create an urgent feeling to close the document and walk away. The rule this project follows: if everything is urgent, nothing is.

Use for

Specific deadlines, delivery methods, and numbers or dates that trigger legal obligations.

Use for

Risk indicators. Red borders around clauses that shift significant liability, reserved for the highest risk provisions only.

Do not use red for headers, titles, or full paragraphs. It reduces readability.
Do not use red in visuals unless denoting loss, risk, or non-compliance.
Do not use red for reviewer comments. It desensitizes readers to its real alert function.
Psychology of Color

Yellow. Notice, process, remember.

Like red, but with better people skills. It captures attention without triggering the stress response red does.

Yellow
Pilot observation, not peer reviewed
0%Recall rate for yellow-highlighted clauses, small sample
0 m/sMedian recognition time, internal test set

Warm colors, yellow in particular, are associated in the literature with attention capture without the stress response red tends to trigger, which makes it well suited to cognitive engagement rather than alarm. It does not yell, and that distinction matters in legal design.

Yellow does not warn. It guides.

A clause buried in paragraph forty seven goes unread. Highlight the same line in yellow, and people suddenly see what they are entitled to, not just what they are stuck with.

Highlight user choice

Optional actions, rights, and non-mandatory elements. Where readers actually have power.

Reveal hidden consequences

Auto-renewals, interest charges, the quiet risks people skim past.

Emphasize benefits

Shift the document from what you cannot do to what you get.

Do not highlight entire paragraphs. It strains the eyes and kills readability.
Do not pair yellow with white or light backgrounds. It disappears.
Do not use yellow for deadlines or terminations. That is red's job.
